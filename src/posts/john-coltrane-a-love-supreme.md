Everyone agrees that *A Love Supreme* is great. The disagreement — usually unspoken — is about what kind of great it is.

The standard framing is spiritual: Coltrane in 1964, recently clean, writing a prayer in four movements. That framing isn't wrong, but it can make the album feel more like a monument than a record. Something to respect rather than to love.

## The Sound Before the Concept

Before the liner notes, before the four-note motif that becomes a mantra, there's just the opening: a gong, a bass, and Elvin Jones's cymbal shimmer building for thirty seconds before anything else enters.

It doesn't feel like a prayer. It feels like weather changing.

## What Elvin Jones Actually Does

The attention in this record usually goes to Coltrane — the soprano sax phrases that spiral and resolve, the harmonic density. But Elvin Jones is the reason it works. His polyrhythmic drumming creates the feeling that the music is always slightly ahead of where you thought it was going. That displacement is what makes the album feel alive rather than composed.

## The Part Nobody Talks About

**Psalm**, the fourth movement, is Coltrane playing the liner note prayer — not literally, but syllable-for-syllable on the saxophone. It's a spoken text translated into horn language. Most people can't hear it explicitly, but they feel the difference. The music sounds like language because it is.

That's what "spiritual" actually means here: a specific technical decision made in service of an emotional and theological purpose. Not vague transcendence. Precise construction.
