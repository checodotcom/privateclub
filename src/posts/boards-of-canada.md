There's a particular feeling that only a few records manage to produce: the sensation of remembering something that never happened. **Music Has the Right to Children** by Boards of Canada is the most complete realization of that feeling I know.

## The Tape Hiss as Aesthetic

Marcus Eoin and Michael Sandison grew up filming Super 8 footage in the Scottish countryside. That biographical detail matters because their music sounds exactly like those films: warm, slightly overexposed, the colors a little wrong. The tape hiss isn't a flaw — it's the whole point.

> "The music sounds like it's coming from a room you once knew and can no longer find."

The drum machines are just slightly off-tempo. The samples breathe in ways digital recordings don't. Everything decays.

## Why It Still Holds Up

Released in 1998, the record doesn't sound dated because it never sounded current. It sounded already-old the day it came out. That's the trick. Music that sounds like the past ages better than music that tries to sound like the present.

Tracks like *Roygbiv* and *Aquarius* create a kind of emotional weather — you don't listen to them so much as you get caught in them.

## Recommended Entry Points

- Start with **Roygbiv** — two minutes of perfect drum programming
- Then **Aquarius** — the long arc of a Sunday afternoon in 1984
- End with **Olson** — a children's show theme from a country that doesn't exist

This record changed what I thought music could do. It still does.
